# [Modulation Identification] Distributed Deep Learning for Modulation Classification in 6G Uniform Cellular Wireless Networks [with Python Code]

In the evolution of 6G technology, the emergence of uncellular networks has brought about paradigm shifts. The collaboration of distributed access points provides a revolutionary approach to enhancing user experience in densely deployed networks. However, integrating intelligent mechanisms is crucial for optimizing the efficiency, scalability, and adaptability of these 6G uncellular networks. Automated Modulation Classification (AMC) is an application aimed at optimizing spectrum usage, serving as a key component for classification and dynamically adjusting modulation schemes. This paper explores different distributed solutions for AMC in uncellular networks, examining two practical methods for training, computational complexity, and accuracy. The first method addresses scenarios where signals cannot be shared due to privacy concerns or pre-averaging limitations, achieving considerable accuracy but with higher computational demands; the second method considers a hybrid model that collaborates with multiple distributed models for modulation classification. This hybrid model leverages diversity gain through signal aggregation, requiring synchronization and signal sharing. It can improve accuracy by 2.5% while distributing the computational load across multiple devices, reducing the computational pressure on individual devices.
The introduction highlights that future advancements in 6G mobile networks are poised to revolutionize wireless communication, steering towards more densely deployed and embedded AI applications [1]. These future networks will utilize multiple frequency bands, combining various numerical and modulation techniques at the physical layer, employing diverse distributed precoding, beamforming, or receiving combining methods [2,3]. In such distributed networks, advanced perception and low-cost control solutions are required to optimize resource allocation.
In the context of 6G, we are witnessing a trend towards uncellular distributed deployment [4]. To overcome path loss and provide uniform signal-to-noise ratio (SNR) for all user locations, a dense distribution of transmitters and receivers is a good strategy known as favorable path loss conditions [5]. For this purpose, an antenna allocation strategy to optimize the combination of signals from multiple locations is essential. Therefore, distributing computational workload between different network components is crucial for ensuring the scalability of the distributed network.
One popular practical variant of an uncellular network is based on the Open Radio Access Network (O-RAN) architecture [6], where the allocation of computational workload is a key aspect. This allocation is reflected in breaking down the network architecture into Central Units (CU), Distributed Units (DU), and Radio Units (RU). In this paper, we adopt this terminology, assigning the responsibility of low-layer physical layer processing to RU while DU handles the final physical layer processing. CU is not discussed within the scope of this article. Specifically, our focus is on multiple receivers in a distributed environment, which aligns well with the evolving 6G uncellular system environment.
Numerous studies, such as [7], have explored perception position diversity and consensus methods among multiple receivers beyond the 6G context. Projects like RadioHound [8] and Electrosense [9] highlight the rise of distributed perception. However, it should be clear that although perception is distributed, processing remains centralized. Automotive Modulation Classification (AMC) has been an exploration point for more flexible and cognitive networks since it was introduced as a key technology for wireless networks around 15 years ago [10,11]. Since then, related methods have shifted from statistical signal processing to deep learning-based models [12]. The vast research in the field of Deep Learning (DL)-AMC has mainly focused on centralized models, where the ResNet [13] architecture is widely considered one of the best feasible architectures for performance.
The study of models that incorporate multiple inputs and explore the benefits of ensemble gain has received considerable attention, providing valuable insights into this field. However, research on fully distributed models with multiple input signals is relatively scarce. Although some papers, such as [15], provide preliminary insights, a comprehensive understanding of the impact of accuracy and computational complexity is evidently lacking. To our knowledge, there remains a research gap in integrating AMC into distributed uncellular networks, which is the main motivation for this paper. This paper fills this gap by analyzing the impact on performance and total computational load, focusing on two different AMC distributed methods.
Article Illustration
Conclusion: This paper introduces two novel distributed methods for automatic modulation classification (AMC) in uncellular networks, and evaluates these models based on accuracy and computational complexity. The distributed models address scenarios where IQ samples cannot be shared due to forwarding limitations or privacy considerations compared to centralized models that require strict synchronization between RUs. These distributed models can achieve similar complexity without synchronization constraints, but their computational requirements are several times higher than those of centralized models. When EGC is available at DU, the hybrid model strikes a balance between accuracy and complexity, providing efficient workload allocation and outperforming centralized models.
Our work establishes a foundational framework for AMC in uncellular networks, paving new research directions, including exploring more complex channels other than AWGN, alternative signal combination methods beyond EGC for RUs, and exploring distributed AMC architectures other than ResNet.
